# <center> Unibuy DEX Protocol v0.2 </center>

<center> Derik Lu, ldru0519@gmail.com, May 28, 2026</center>

## Abstract

Unibuy is an order book-like exchange protocol based on the constant-product formula. In a centralized exchange order book, prices are user-defined single points; in the Unibuy order book, prices are specified as price ranges along the constant-product curve.

The Unibuy protocol sets up two mirrored one-way liquidity pools for each token pair, providing a trading experience similar to that of Uniswap’s two-way liquidity pools. The effect of this one-way pair design is that a small price spread is automatically maintained between the two mirrored pairs, which effectively mitigates sandwich attacks, a common problem in current DEX trading.

User orders are generally divided into two types: maker orders and taker orders. A maker order sells a specified amount of $\small token_0$ within a specified price range. The lower bound of the price range, after mirroring, must be lower than the current price of the associated mirror pair, ensuring that the maker order can not be filled immediately. A taker order buys a specified amount of $\small token_0$ or sells a specified amount of $\small token_1$, provided that its highest acceptable price is above the current price. Taker orders are always filled immediately.

A one-way trading pair always sells $\small token_0$ and buys $\small token_1$. In the mirror pair, the definitions of $\small token_0$ and $\small token_1$ are reversed. From the perspective of a maker-order user, a maker order always sells $\small token_0$ and buys $\small token_1$. From the perspective of a taker-order user, it sells $\small token_1$ and buys $\small token_0$. Maker orders and taker orders act as counterparties to one another, and the buying and selling of the same token are completed through two linked mirror trading pairs.

Taker orders always drive the price of $\small token_0$ upward along the constant-product curve, whereas maker orders may drive the price of the current trading pair downward. If the token price later reverses after a maker order has been fully or partially filled, the maker order does not passively buy back the sold $\small token_0$, unlike liquidity in the Uniswap protocol. This design addresses the impermanent-loss problem faced by liquidity providers in Uniswap.

The Unibuy protocol introduces a range-liquidity rebalancing mechanism and a forward compensation mechanism for maker orders. These mechanisms resolve the internal arbitrage opportunities that may arise in maker-order transactions and allow maker-order liquidity across different price ranges to be aggregated.

Liquidity in the Unibuy protocol is provided entirely by maker orders. There is no automated market maker (AMM) role as in Uniswap, so all trading fees paid by taker orders become protocol revenue for Unibuy.

## 1. Introduction

The Uniswap protocol$^{[1,2,3,4]}$ has undergone continuous upgrades from v1/v2 to v3/v4, steadily improving the implementation of the Constant Product Automated Market Maker (CPAMM) mechanism. These upgrades have increased capital efficiency for liquidity providers and established Uniswap as the leading DEX in DeFi. Its core design has been widely imitated by many Web3 projects. However, Uniswap-type DEXs also face several issues.

### 1.1 Protocol Revenue Issue

The Uniswap protocol requires liquidity providers to supply liquidity for user transactions. In return, liquidity providers receive most of the transaction fees paid by users, while the DEX project itself receives only a small portion. Uniswap includes a switch that enables protocol fee sharing, but it can be activated only through governance voting. In practice, this has led to situations in which Uniswap is unable to generate meaningful revenue from user transactions.

### 1.2 Impermanent Loss Issue

Although the constant-product AMM mechanism solves the problem of automatic pricing in token trading, it inevitably introduces the difficult problem of impermanent loss$^{[5]}$. Professional market makers can partially mitigate impermanent loss by continuously monitoring trading pairs and dynamically adjusting their liquidity distribution in response to price changes. Ordinary Web3 users, however, typically lack the tools and expertise to do so. If they provide liquidity for long periods without timely adjustments, the limited returns from market making often fail to offset the impermanent loss they suffer. This discourages ordinary users from participating as liquidity providers and negatively affects the overall liquidity of DEXs.

### 1.3 Sandwich Attack Issue

Uniswap trading pairs are bidirectional, meaning that the selling price of a token is also its buying price. After a user executes a trade, the token price moves predictably along the constant-product curve, making the transaction vulnerable to sandwich attacks. In such an attack, a malicious actor front-runs a user’s large trade by placing a transaction in the same direction, causing the token price to move unfavorably for the user. After the user’s transaction is executed, the attacker places a reverse transaction and captures arbitrage profit from the price spread. Large numbers of such attacks can be observed on-chain. Although many countermeasures have been proposed$^{[6,7]}$, none is universally applicable.

The Unibuy protocol can natively solve all three of the issues above through its design.

### 1.4 Unibuy Protocol Solutions

The Unibuy protocol adopts an order book mechanism similar to that of centralized exchanges (CEXs), eliminating the role of liquidity providers as market makers. All trading fees are retained by the Unibuy protocol operators, which inherently solves the problem of transaction-fee allocation.

As in a CEX order book, liquidity in the Unibuy protocol is provided by users’ maker orders waiting to be filled. Once a taker order is executed, the received $\small token_1$ leaves the trading flow immediately rather than being reused in further trades, as it would be in a constant-product market maker. Because there is no market-maker role, the protocol naturally avoids impermanent loss caused by large price movements.

The Unibuy protocol uses unidirectional trading pairs because of its order-book design. To support two-way token trading, two mirrored unidirectional pairs are deployed. This internal technical design does not affect the user trading experience. However, it creates a useful side effect: the buy and sell prices for the same token in the mirrored pairs naturally maintain a spread. That inherent spread effectively protects against sandwich attacks. Even if an attacker front-runs a trade, they cannot manipulate the token price in the mirrored pair and therefore cannot profit from a reverse transaction, rendering the attack economically pointless.

### 1.5 Differences from CEX Order Books

Despite the similarities, the Unibuy protocol’s order book differs from that of centralized exchanges.

In a CEX, when a user places a buy or sell order, it is immediately filled if there is a matching order in the order book, this is a taker order. If no match is found, the order is added to the order book and displayed to all traders, waiting to be filled with coming orders. Each order has a single fixed price, and the entire order book forms a set of discrete price points on the price axis.

In contrast, Unibuy orders specify a price range rather than a single price. Within that range, the specified token amount is automatically filled according to the constant-product curve. The Unibuy protocol automatically determines, based on user-defined parameters, whether an order becomes a taker order, a maker order, or a partial fill. If an order can only be partially filled, the unfilled portion can either be converted into a maker order awaiting a future taker or be terminated immediately after the partial fill, depending on the user’s preference. The liquidity of maker orders across different price ranges is aggregated to form the liquidity available to taker orders.

Unibuy does not adopt the typical CEX price-time-priority execution rule. Instead, it aggregates all maker-order liquidity and fills taker orders according to the constant-product formula.

Unibuy maker orders are publicly visible on-chain and represent real user orders, avoiding issues common to CEXs such as fake orders and iceberg orders that may mislead users. Due to blockchain characteristics such as block-time granularity and transaction competition, placing deceptive orders on-chain carries substantial risk and limited benefit.

CEXs usually incentivize makers with lower trading fees than takers. In the Unibuy protocol, maker orders are always free, whether filled or not.

Some earlier projects, such as EtherDelta$^{[8]}$ and Serum$^{[9]}$, attempted to implement on-chain order-book DEXs. However, those projects largely copied traditional CEX order books onto the chain without sufficient technical innovation or adaptation to blockchain-specific features. For various reasons, they did not achieve broad success. A viable on-chain order book requires technical breakthroughs and genuine innovation.

### 1.6 Unibuy Protocol

Unibuy is a decentralized exchange protocol that provides order-book functionality. By integrating the constant-product AMM mechanism with key features of CEX order books, it aggregates users’ maker orders as liquidity for taker orders without requiring a complex on-chain order-matching process. This approach explores a practical path toward a fully on-chain order-book DEX.

The Unibuy protocol sets up two mirrored one-way trading pairs for each token pair. Each pair supports the sale of one token, and the two one-way trading pairs are linked together to provide two-way trading for the token pair.

The idea of using two mirrored unidirectional pairs in a DEX was previously explored by FeSwap$^{[10]}$, which used this design to offer a zero-fee DEX with market-making returns. Although that project lacked broad promotion and a large user base, it continued to operate and accumulated a meaningful amount of on-chain trading activity. While Unibuy’s mirrored-pair design appears similar on the surface, it is fundamentally different in its technical implementation and is specifically designed to enable a sound on-chain order book.

To aggregate maker-order liquidity from different users, price ranges, and order batches, Unibuy introduces a liquidity rebalancing mechanism and a forward compensation mechanism for maker orders. These mechanisms allow maker-order liquidity to be aggregated for taker orders without requiring a complex CEX-style order-matching process or settlement system. The following sections describe Unibuy’s technical design in detail.

## 2. Unibuy DEX Protocol

### 2.1 Basics of Range Liquidity

In Uniswap v3/v4, to improve the capital efficiency of liquidity provision, liquidity providers (LPs) can specify the price range within which they provide liquidity. This avoids allocating capital to price intervals that are unlikely to be reached, thereby avoiding wasted capital. Uniswap introduces the concept of $\small tick$, which is a discrete integer corresponding to token prices in a trading pair. Using $\small ticks$, liquidity can be divided into multiple distinct price ranges, allowing liquidity within the same range to aggregate and participate collectively in token trading.

When the price of a trading pair moves and crosses into a new price range, the protocol adjusts the liquidity parameters accordingly, so that in each range the trading pair still follows the constant-product formula, but with different liquidity constants.

By using $\small ticks$ to define the price range for liquidity provision, LPs need to allocate capital only to a specific price interval. Compared with Uniswap v1/v2, which requires liquidity over the full range of $\small (0, \infty)$, this approach allows the same amount of capital to support more effective trading liquidity. This is the core idea behind Uniswap’s concept of concentrated, or virtual, liquidity.

Figure 1 illustrates a price range, also known as a $\small tick$ interval, denoted by $\small (P_a, P_b)$. Assuming the virtual liquidity constant is $\small L$, and the virtual amounts of the two tokens in the trading pair are $\small x, y$, the constant-product formula is expressed as:

$$ 
  \small x * y = L^2 \tag{2.1.1} 
$$

Formally, this formula has the same expression as the constant-product formula used in Uniswap v1/v2, but its meaning is different. In Uniswap v1/v2, $\small x, y$ represent the real amounts of the two tokens, and the constant $\small L$ remains unchanged across the full price space.

However, in Uniswap v3/v4 and in the Unibuy protocol, $\small x$ and $\small y$ are virtual token amounts, and the constant $\small L$ remains unchanged only within a given $\small tick$ interval. Across different tick intervals, the value of $\small L$ usually changes.

Within the Unibuy protocol, what is operationally significant is the change in the virtual token amounts across the price range, denoted by $\small \Delta x$ and $\small \Delta y$, which satisfy the following formulas:

$$ 
  \small \Delta x = (\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_a}}- \frac{1}{\sqrt{P_b}}) * L \tag{2.1.2} 
$$

$$ 
  \small \Delta y = (\sqrt{P_b}- \sqrt{P_a}) * L \tag{2.1.3} 
$$

$$ 
  \small \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \sqrt{P_a * P_b }   \tag{2.1.4} 
$$

In the Unibuy protocol, when a user places a maker order to sell an amount $\small \Delta x$ of an asset within the price range $\small (P_a, P_b)$, the corresponding virtual liquidity $\small L$ for that range can be calculated using equation (2.1.2). The protocol records that virtual liquidity internally. By simply adding the virtual liquidity contributed by different users within the same price range, the protocol aggregates range liquidity.

When a user places a taker order, the virtual liquidity constant $\small L$ is already known. The price change of the trading pair can therefore be calculated from the amount $\small \Delta y$ the user wishes to sell or the amount $\small \Delta x$ the user wishes to buy, using equation (2.1.3) or (2.1.2), respectively, thereby determining the corresponding $\small \Delta x$ or $\small \Delta y$.

Equation (2.1.4) indicates that when an amount $\small \Delta x$ of one token is swapped for an amount $\small \Delta y$ of another token within the price range $\small (P_a, P_b)$, the average exchange price is $\small \sqrt{P_a * P_b}$.

### 2.2 One-Sided Liquidity

The Unibuy protocol inherits the virtual-liquidity design from Uniswap v3/v4, where all liquidity is divided into different tick intervals, that is, price ranges. Within a given tick interval, the virtual liquidity constant $\small L$ remains unchanged. During trading, when the price of the token pair changes and crosses a tick boundary, the liquidity constant $\small L$ is adjusted according to the information recorded for the crossed tick.

However, there are major differences between the Unibuy protocol and the Uniswap protocol. In Uniswap, trading within a tick interval is bidirectional, whereas in Unibuy, trading within a tick interval is unidirectional. This difference means that in Unibuy, liquidity exists only above the current price of the trading pair, while in Uniswap v3/v4 liquidity can be distributed across the entire price range. The figure below illustrates the liquidity distribution in the Unibuy protocol.

In the Uniswap protocol, the same trading pair supports mutual exchange between $\small token_0$ and $\small token_1$. Liquidity providers supply liquidity for trading, and users pay a transaction fee of approximately $\small 0.3\%$. Liquidity providers earn that fee, while Uniswap takes a small portion of it, or in some cases, none at all.

In the Unibuy protocol, there is no liquidity-provider role. All trading liquidity comes from users’ maker orders. Maker orders require only one type of token, defined in the protocol as $\small token_0$. Taker orders always exchange $\small token_1$ for $\small token_0$ from the trading pair.

The economic effect of buying or selling $\small token_0/token_1$ is actually implemented through two interconnected mirrored unidirectional trading pairs. The operations of adding liquidity, removing liquidity, and swapping in Uniswap correspond to placing a maker order, closing a maker order, and placing a taker order in Unibuy. From the user’s perspective, Unibuy offers a trading experience closer to that of centralized exchanges.

In the Unibuy protocol, taker orders incur a transaction fee, while maker orders are free regardless of whether they are filled. All transaction fees paid by taker orders belong entirely to the Unibuy protocol.

### 2.3 One-Way Trading Pairs

A DEX protocol must provide two-way trading. In the Unibuy protocol, that two-way functionality is implemented through two linked mirrored unidirectional trading pairs. In these two linked pairs, the definitions of $\small token_0$ and $\small token_1$ are reversed. For example, given two assets, Token A and Token B, if Token A is $\small token_0$ and Token B is $\small token_1$ in one trading pair, then in the mirrored pair Token A becomes $\small token_1$ and Token B becomes $\small token_0$.

Because of this symmetry, the logic of both mirrored pairs is identical: taker-side users always use $\small token_1$ to exchange for the $\small token_0$ being sold through maker orders. These mirrored unidirectional pairs are simply an internal implementation solution. Users placing maker orders and taker orders do not need to be aware of it, and it does not affect the user experience.

When placing a maker order, users specify a price range and the amount of $\small token_0$ to sell. All maker orders are aggregated to form the trading pair’s liquidity. When a user places a taker order, that user’s counterparty is the aggregate liquidity of maker orders. Since taker orders always exchange $\small token_1$ for $\small token_0$, the price of $\small token_0$ always rises unidirectionally.

The price range specified in a maker order may lie above or below the current price of the trading pair. However, the lower bound of the price range, after mirroring, must not exceed the current price of the mirrored trading pair, which is the reciprocal of the current price in the original pair. Otherwise, that portion of the order could be filled immediately in the mirrored pair.

The Unibuy protocol checks the user-specified price range against the current state of the trading pair to determine whether to process the order as a taker order or a maker order. If necessary, the protocol can partially handle the order as a taker order and then treat the unfilled portion as a maker order.

If the lower bound of a maker order’s price range is below the current price of the trading pair, that lower bound becomes the new current price of the trading pair after transaction, and then serves as the starting price for subsequent taker orders. However, if the lower bound is above the current price, the trading pair’s current price remains unchanged.

## 3. Unibuy Internal Mechanisms

While the design of Unibuy’s one-way trading pairs effectively addresses issues such as sandwich attacks and protocol revenue, it also introduces additional complexity in aggregating liquidity from different maker orders and in correctly calculating the settlement amounts of filled maker orders. The Unibuy protocol therefore introduces several mechanisms to manage this complexity, mainly the liquidity rebalancing mechanism and the internal-exchange compensation mechanism.

### 3.1 Liquidity Rebalancing Mechanism

When a user places a maker order with a price range $\small (P_l, P_u)$ and the lower bound $\small P_l$ is below the current price $\small P_0$ of the trading pair, the liquidity rebalancing mechanism is triggered. The reason is that within the current price range of the trading pair, some liquidity may already have been partially filled. If the price range of the new maker order overlaps with that current range, the two sets of liquidity cannot be aggregated directly. The partially filled liquidity must first be rebalanced into an appropriate price range before aggregation can occur. In addition, once the new maker order is placed, $\small P_l$ becomes the new current price of the trading pair if $\small P_l \leqslant P_0$, which likewise requires the existing liquidity in the current price range to be rebalanced for future aggregation.

The newly added liquidity, relative to the current price range, can fall into 10 different cases, as illustrated in Figure 3. Among these:

Cases 1/2/5: Since $\small P_l > P_0$, no rebalancing is triggered.

The remaining cases require rebalancing as follows:

Cases 4/7/10: rebalance to the lower bound of the current liquidity range, $\small P_a$.

Cases 8/9: rebalance to the upper bound of the new liquidity range, $\small P_u$.

Cases 3/6: rebalance to the lower bound of the new liquidity range, $\small P_l$.

After rebalancing, the upper bound $\small P_b$ of the price range remains unchanged. The lower bound of the rebalanced price range, denoted by $\small P_r$, is determined by the following formula:

$$
  P_r = \left\{ 
  \begin{aligned} 
  \ \ &P_u    &(tick_u \geqslant tick_a) \ \& \ (tick_u \leqslant tick_0) \\
  \ \ &P_l    &(tick_l \geqslant tick_a) \ \& \ (tick_l \leqslant tick_0) \\
  \ \ &P_a    &others \\
  \end{aligned} 
  \right. \tag{3.1.1} 
$$ 

where $\small tick_a, tick_b, tick_0, tick_u, tick_l$ are the $\small tick$ values corresponding to the prices $\small P_a, P_b, P_0, P_u, P_l$, respectively. This formula applies only when $\small tick_l \leqslant tick_0$; no rebalancing is needed when $\small tick_l > tick_0$.

In the formula above, the evaluation priority for $\small P_r$ is $\small P_u \gt P_l \gt P_a$. In other words, if the condition for $\small P_u$ is satisfied, then $\small P_r = P_u$; otherwise, if the condition for $\small P_l$ is satisfied, then $\small P_r = P_l$; otherwise, $\small P_r = P_a$.

When $\small P_r = P_u$ or $\small P_r = P_l$, the current liquidity range must first be split, and the liquidity in $\small (P_a, P_r)$ is treated as fully filled.

Assume that the current price of the trading pair is $\small P_0$, that the new price range after rebalancing is $\small (P_{r}, \ P_{b})$, and that the liquidity of the current price range is $\small L_1$. Since the current price is $\small P_{0}$, only the portion between $\small (P_0, P_b)$ still has liquidity; the portion between $\small (P_a, P_0)$ has already been sold. The purpose of rebalancing is to redistribute the unsold $\small token_0$ across the full new price range $\small (P_{r}, P_{b})$ so that it can be aggregated with the newly added liquidity. After rebalancing, because the amount of $\small token_0$ available for sale remains unchanged, formula $\small (2.1.2)$ implies that the new virtual liquidity $\small L_2$ satisfies:

$$
  \begin{aligned} (\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_{r}}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{P_{b}}}) * L_{2} = (\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_{0}}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{P_{b}}}) * L_1 \end{aligned} \tag{3.1.2}
$$

$$
  \begin{aligned} L_{2} = \frac{\sqrt{P_{b}}-\sqrt{P_{0}}}{\sqrt{P_{0}}} * \frac{\sqrt{P_{r}}}{\sqrt{P_{b}}-\sqrt{P_{r}}} * L_1 \end{aligned} \tag{3.1.3}
$$

And also have:

$$
  \begin{aligned} L_{1} - L_{2} = \frac{\sqrt{P_{0}}-\sqrt{P_{r}}}{\sqrt{P_{0}}} * \frac{\sqrt{P_{b}}}{\sqrt{P_{b}}-\sqrt{P_{r}}} * L_1 \end{aligned} \tag{3.1.4}
$$

Rebalancing redistributes the remaining unsold liquidity in the current price range to the new full price range $\small (P_r, P_b)$. The reduction in net liquidity after rebalancing is $\small L_1 - L_2$.

The rebalancing mechanism affects not only the liquidity of the current price range but also the price of the trading pair. After rebalancing, the lower bound of the newly added liquidity becomes the new current price of the trading pair. If no rebalancing occurs, the trading pair’s price remains unchanged. In the Unibuy protocol, users’ taker orders always cause the price of the trading pair to move upward, whereas users’ maker orders may cause it to fall through the addition of lower-priced liquidity.

Due to the design of the rebalancing mechanism, a user’s liquidity within the price range $\small (P_a, P_b)$ is not guaranteed to be sold at the average price $\small \sqrt{P_a * P_b}$. After multiple rounds of rebalancing, the actual average selling price $\small P_m$ of $\small token_0$ satisfies $\small P_a < P_m \leqslant \sqrt{P_a * P_b}$. This design allows liquidity to trade as quickly as possible within the specified price range while still preserving favorable pricing under the constraints of the constant-product curve.

## 3.2 Internal exchange Compensation Mechanism

When a user places a maker order, the newly added liquidity is aggregated with existing liquidity to serve as the counterparty of taker orders. However, the existing liquidity in that price range may have already been partially filled, and its remaining $\small token_0$ has been redistributed across the entire price range via the rebalancing mechanism. However, the newly added liquidity is completely unfilled within that price range, and therefore has a different intrinsic value from the existing liquidity. Furthermore, due to the constraints of the constant product pricing mechanism, these two types of liquidity must be aggregated together and treated uniformly for the processing of taker orders. To resolve this issue, the Unibuy protocol introduces the internal exchange compensation mechanism. While recognizing the value advantage of earlier maker orders, this mechanism ensures that liquidity from different orders with different trading states own an internally consistent value, and can be uniformly involved in taker order processing and the correct calculation of the intrinsic value of different orders when settling each maker order.

This internal exchange compensation mechanism applies only to price ranges that have been partially filled. Price ranges that are entirely unfilled or fully filled do not require compensation processing.
 
Assume that before new liquidity is added, the total liquidity in the price range is $\small L_1$, which has already been partially traded, and the amount of $\small token_1$ received is $\small R$. After rebalancing, the remaining net liquidity is $\small L_2$. Let the newly added liquidity be $\small L_d$. After the new liquidity is added, the total liquidity in the range is $\small L_1 + L_d$, the total net liquidity is $\small L_2 + L_d$, and the amount of $\small token_1$ received remains $\small R$. The states are summarized in the following table:

$$ 
  \small \newcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8} \begin{array}  {| c ||  c | c | c |}
  \hline
  Range\ liquidity\ status & Total\ Liquidity & Net\ Liquidity & Token_1\ Amount\\
  \hline
  Before\ adding\ liquidity &{L_1} &L_2 &R \\
  \hline
  After\ adding\ liquidity &{L_1 + L_d} &{L_2 + L_d} &R \\
  \hline  
  After\ withdrawing\ liquidity &{L_1} &{\dfrac{L_1}{L_1 + L_d} * (L_2 + L_d)} &{\dfrac{L_1}{L_1 + L_d}} * R \\[0.15cm]
  \hline  
 \end{array}
 $$

Since a user’s asset entitlement is always calculated in proportion to that user’s share of total liquidity, if a later user (hereinafter referred to as user $\small 2$) adds liquidity and then immediately withdraws it, the amount of $\small token_1$ that user would receive, denoted by $\small R_x$, is:

$$
  \begin{aligned} \small R_x = \frac{L_d}{L_1+ L_d} * R \end{aligned} \tag{3.2.1}
$$

The withdrawn liquidity would be $\small L_d * (L_2+ L_d) / (L_1+ L_d)$. Compared with the initial liquidity $\small L_d$ added by user $\small 2$, the liquidity has decreased. The amount of the reduction, denoted by $\small L_x$, is:

$$
  \begin{aligned} \small L_x = \frac{L_d}{L_1 + L_d} * (L_1 - L_2) \end{aligned} \tag{3.2.2} 
$$

This reduction in liquidity corresponds to a reduction in $\small token_0$, meaning that user $\small 2$ has effectively exchanged part of their $\small token_0$ for an amount $\small R_x$ of $\small token_1$. This is referred to as an internal exchange. The amount of $\small token_0$ corresponding to the reduced liquidity $\small L_x$, denoted by $\small S_x$ (equivalent to the amount of $\small token_0$ effectively exchanged when user $\small 2$ withdraws immediately), is:

$$
  \begin{aligned} \small S_x \ = \ &(\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_r}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{P_b}}) * L_x \\[3mm]
  = \ &\frac{L_d}{L_1 + L_d} * (\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_r}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{P_0}}) * L_1 \\
  \end{aligned} \tag{3.2.3} 
$$

After withdrawing successfully, user $\small 2$ has effectively exchanged $\small S_x$ of $\small token_0$, corresponding to liquidity $\small L_x$ within the price range $\small (P_r, P_b)$, for $\small R_x$ of $\small token_1$. Since the liquidity value of $\small (L_1 - L_2)$ in terms of $\small token_0$ is equivalent to an amount $\small R$ of $\small token_1$, this internal exchange is value-equivalent and therefore reasonable. It is easy to verify that $\small R_x = S_x * \sqrt{P_r * P_0}$, where $\small \sqrt{P_r * P_0}$ is the average price of this internal exchange between $\small token_0$ and $\small token_1$.

Accordingly, after the order is withdrawn, the liquidity in this price range has increased by $\small L_x$ relative to the liquidity $\small L_2$ before the new order was added. However, the amount of $\small token_1$ becomes $\small R * L_1 / (L_1 + L_d)$, which is reduced by $\small R_x$ relative to the original amount $\small R$. This is equivalent to the liquidity range exchanging $\small R_x$ of $\small token_1$ for an equal-value amount of $\small token_0$ liquidity in the same range.

Although this internal exchange is value-equivalent based on the state of the trading pair at the time of liquidity rebalancing, it is unfair to the earlier maker-order users (collectively referred to hereinafter as user $\small 1$). Whenever the internal exchange is triggered, user $\small 1$’s filled liquidity has necessarily been sold at a price higher than the current pool price or the lower price bound of user $\small 2$’s maker order. Without a reasonable compensation mechanism for user $\small 1$, user $\small 2$ would effectively exchange a certain amount of $\small token_0$ for $\small token_1$ at a price higher than the current pair price, resulting in hidden arbitrage at user $\small 1$’s expense. To address this problem, the Unibuy protocol introduces an internal exchange compensation mechanism that requires user $\small 2$ to compensate user $\small 1$ according to defined rules. This mechanism removes the unfairness of hidden arbitrage and gives user $\small 1$ a first-mover advantage. On centralized exchanges, orders are matched according to time priority, which gives earlier orders a first-mover advantage. In the Unibuy protocol, user orders are not prioritized by placement time; instead, that first-mover advantage is realized through the internal exchange compensation mechanism.

Compensation in the Unibuy protocol has two components: internal exchange fee compensation, denoted by $C_a$, and internal exchange price-difference compensation, denoted by $C_b/C_c$. They are introduced below.

### 3.3 Internal Exchange Fee Compensation

The Unibuy protocol requires the later maker-order user $\small 2$ to pay internal exchange fee compensation according to the configured internal exchange compensation rate and the internal exchange amount. The compensation is denominated in $\small token_1$. If the compensation fee rate is $\small r$, the internal exchange fee compensation amount is:

$$ 
  \small \begin{aligned} C^{'}_{a} = r * \frac{L_{d}}{L_{1} + L_{d}} * R \end{aligned} \tag{3.3.1}
$$

Because internal exchange fee compensation is distributed pro rata across all liquidity recorded in $\small tickInfo$, including the liquidity of user $\small 2$, the nominal compensation paid by user $\small 2$ must be:

$$ 
  \small \begin{aligned} C_{a} = r * \frac{L_{d}}{L_{1}} * R \end{aligned} \tag{3.3.2}
$$

It is easy to verify that the amount of $\small token_1$ received pro rata by user $\small 1$ is:

$$ 
  \small \begin{aligned} \frac{L_{1}}{L_{1} + L_{d}} * (R + C_{a}) = \frac{L_{1}}{L_{1} + L_{d}} * R + r * \frac{L_{d}}{L_{1} + L_{d}} * R \end{aligned} \tag{3.3.3}
$$

The first term is the $\small token_1$ amount user $\small 1$ would receive without compensation, and the second term is the internal exchange fee compensation obtained by user $\small 1$, namely $\small C^{'}_{a}$ in $\small (3.3.1)$.

The amount of $\small token_1$ received pro rata by user $\small 2$ is:

$$ 
  \small \begin{aligned} \frac{L_{d}}{L_{1} + L_{d}} * (R + C_{a}) - C_a = \frac{L_{d}}{L_{1} + L_{d}} * R - r * \frac{L_{d}}{L_{1} + L_{d}} * R \end{aligned} \tag{3.3.4}
$$

The second term in the expression above is precisely the internal exchange fee compensation paid by user $\small 2$. In the Unibuy implementation, the amount actually recorded and processed is $\small C_{a}$, not $\small C^{'}_{a}$. The internal exchange compensation fee rate is generally set to $\small 0.2\%$.

### 3.4 Internal Exchange Price-Difference Compensation

As described above, if the price range of a new order overlaps with that of an already partially filled order, a compensation mechanism is required to prevent unreasonable arbitrage opportunities. When new and existing liquidity are aggregated, the implied internal exchange amount, denominated in $\small token_1$, is $\small R_x$ as defined in section $\small (3.2.1)$.

The Unibuy protocol specifies that this internal exchange is valued at the current price $\small P_0$ of the trading pair. Here, $\small P_0$ refers to the price after the maker order is added to the trading pair, specifically the lower of the order’s lower price bound and the trading pair’s current price at the time of order placement.

Although the average trade price of $\small R_x$ is $\small \sqrt{P_a * P_x}$, where $\small P_x$ is the trading pair’s price $\small P_0$ at the time of liquidity rebalancing and $\small P_a$ is the $\small P_r$ mentioned in section (3.1), $\small P_x$ is uncertain. Unibuy therefore uses the upper-bound price $\small P_b$ of the price range $\small (P_a, P_b)$ to calculate the nominal value of $\small R_x$.

Because an arbitrage opportunity arises from the difference between $\small P_0$ and $\small \sqrt{P_a * P_b}$, user $\small 2$ must compensate the earlier liquidity providers through internal exchange price-difference compensation. If this compensation is denoted by $\small C_b$, then $\small C_b$ should satisfy the following formula:

$$ 
  \small \begin{aligned} \frac{P_0}{\sqrt{P_a* P_b}} = \frac{\cfrac{L_d}{L_1 + L_d} *(R+C_b) - C_b}{\cfrac{L_d}{L_1 + L_d} * R}  \end{aligned} \tag{3.4.1}
$$

The logic of this equation is that, after applying price-difference compensation, the ratio of user $\small 2$’s actual $\small token_1$ amount received to its nominal $\small token_1$ amount should match the price ratio $\small P_0 / \sqrt{P_a * P_b}$. Simplifying the equation yields:

$$ 
  \small \begin{aligned} C_b = (1 - \frac{P_0}{\sqrt{P_a* P_b}}) * {\cfrac{L_d}{L_1} * R}  \end{aligned} \tag{3.4.2}
$$

Now consider the case in which the partially filled liquidity range has already received some compensation. How should the internal exchange price-difference compensation then be calculated? The following table summarizes that case:

$$ 
  \small \newcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8} \begin{array}  {| c ||  c | c | c | c |}
  \hline
  Range\ liquidity\ status & Total\ Liquidity & Net\ Liquidity & Token_1\ Amount & Compensation \\
  \hline
  Before\ adding\ liquidity &{L_1} &L_2 & R & O \\
  \hline
  After\ adding\ liquidity &{L_1 + L_d} &{L_2 + L_d} & R & O + C_c \\
  \hline  
  After\ withdrawing\ liquidity &{L_1} &{\dfrac{L_1}{L_1 + L_d} * (L_2 + L_d)} & {\dfrac{L_1}{L_1 + L_d}} * R & {\dfrac{L_1}{L_1 + L_d}} * (O + C_c) \\[0.15cm]
  \hline  
 \end{array}
$$

As shown above, the amount of compensation already received is $\small O$. Using logic analogous to formula (3.4.1), we obtain:

$$
  \small \begin{aligned} \frac{P_0}{\sqrt{P_a* P_b}} = \frac{\cfrac{L_d}{L_1 + L_d} *(R + O + C_c) - C_c}{\cfrac{L_d}{L_1 + L_d} * R}  \end{aligned} \tag{3.4.3}
$$

Therefore,

$$
  \small \begin{aligned} C_c = (1 - \frac{P_0}{\sqrt{P_a* P_b}}) * {\cfrac{L_d}{L_1} * R} + {\cfrac{L_d}{L_1} * O}  \end{aligned} \tag{3.4.4}
$$

It is clear that $\small C_c$ reduces exactly to $\small C_b$ when $\small O = 0$.

We also obtain:

$$
  \small \begin{aligned} {\dfrac{L_1}{L_1 + L_d}} * (O + C_c) =  O + (1 - \frac{P_0}{\sqrt{P_a* P_b}}) * {\cfrac{L_d}{L_1 + L_d} * R}  \end{aligned} \tag{3.4.5}
$$

The second term on the left-hand side of formula (3.4.5) is the additional compensation received from the newly added liquidity.

### 3.5 Total Internal Exchange Compensation

$\small C_a$ and $\small C_c$ together make up the total compensation that user $\small 2$ must pay, denoted by $\small C$:

$$ 
  \small \begin{aligned} C = (1 - \frac{P_0}{\sqrt{P_a* P_b}} + r) * {\cfrac{L_d}{L_1} * R} + {\cfrac{L_d}{L_1} * O}  \end{aligned} \tag{3.5}
$$

When a user places a maker order, its price range may overlap with and aggregate into multiple partially filled liquidity ranges. The Unibuy protocol calculates the compensation amounts for all aggregated price ranges associated with that order and records the cumulative compensation amount in the order record. When the user closes or settles the order, the protocol first calculates the total amount of $\small token_1$ the user should receive based on that user’s share of liquidity, then deducts the total compensation amount to determine the final $\small token_1$ amount received by the user.

A price range may receive multiple new maker orders over time, and each time the compensation amount is calculated according to the rules above.

Although internal exchange compensation is included in settlement calculations when a user closes an order, it is zero-sum in nature. The total compensation amount allocated across all liquidity ranges must equal exactly the total amount of internal exchange compensation recorded as deductions in all users’ order information. These amounts offset one another. Internal exchange compensation is therefore only an internal protocol accounting mechanism. It does not affect unrelated liquidity ranges and is completely transparent to taker orders, requiring processing only when a user places or closes a maker order.

### 3.6 Liquidity Range Splitting

When a user places an order, if the given price range $\small (P_l, P_u)$ has its lower and/or upper boundary $\small P_l$ or $\small P_u$ located inside an existing liquidity range $\small (P_a, P_b)$, and that liquidity range is partially filled, then the liquidity range must be split. Specifically, the original range is divided into two sub-ranges, $\small (P_a, P_s)$ and $\small (P_s, P_b)$, where $\small P_s$ is $\small P_u$ and/or $\small P_l$. The purpose of liquidity-range splitting is to enable liquidity aggregation. When splitting, the amount $\small R$ of $\small token_1$ received from the partial fill is divided into upper and lower portions, $\small R_u$ and $\small R_l$, according to the following formulas:

$$
  \begin{aligned} \small 
  R_u \ = \ &\frac{\sqrt{P_b} - \sqrt{P_s}}{\sqrt{P_b} - \sqrt{P_a}} * R \\[3mm]
  R_l \ = \ &R - R_u \\[3mm]
  \end{aligned} \tag{3.6} 
$$

If there is internal exchange compensation in this liquidity range, it must be split according to the same rules.

## 4. Technical Implementation

The following sections introduce the key technical points of the Unibuy protocol at the contract implementation level.

### 4.1 Global State

Within the Unibuy smart contract, each trading pair includes the global state shown in the table below:

$$
  \small \newcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8} \begin{array}  {| c ||  c | c |}
  \hline
  \hspace{0.6cm} Type \hspace{0.6cm} & \hspace{1cm} Variable \hspace{1cm}  &\hspace{0.6cm} Notation \hspace{0.6cm}   \\
  \hline
  uint160  &{sqrtPriceX96}        &{\sqrt{P_0}}           \\
  \hline
  uint24  &{tick}  &{I_t}  \\
  \hline
  uint32  &{poolHeight}        &{H_g}           \\
  \hline
  uint8  &{takerFee}  &{F_t}  \\
  \hline
  uint8  &{offsetFee}  &{F_o}  \\
  \hline
  uint8  &{dayNum}  &{D_n}  \\
  \hline
  uint8  &{tickGapLimit}  &{G_t}  \\
  \hline
  uint8  &{bottomDepth}  &{B_d}  \\
  \hline  
  \end{array}
$$

The trading pair maintains a global height parameter $\small H_g$, whose initial value starts from 1. Each time the trading pair price rises and crosses an upper $\small tick$, $\small H_g$ increases by 1. When a user places a maker order, the current value of $\small H_g$ is recorded in the order information. When the user closes the order, this recorded value and the current $\small H_g$ are used to determine the order's execution status and calculate the proceeds from the maker order.

$\small F_t$ is the fee rate for taker orders. Its default value is 30 basis points, corresponding to a 0.3% trading fee, charged in $\small token_1$.

$\small F_o$ is the internal-exchange compensation fee rate triggered by maker orders, also charged in $\small token_1$.

$\small D_n$ represents an internal date index used to determine whether a fully filled maker order is closed within the required time limit.

$\small G_t$ is the maximum interval for a maker order price range $\small (P_l, P_u)$. Because placing a maker order may trigger rebalancing, range splitting, and compensation processing, a larger price span usually requires more on-chain gas fee consumption. If a user wants to cover a wider overall price range, it can be split into multiple maker orders.

$\small B_d$ is used to store the offset of the lower boundary of the current active range relative to the current tick.

### 4.2 Tick State

Inside the contract, a mapping from a $tick$ index to a $\small tickInfo$ structure is stored. The fields of $\small tickInfo$ are as follows:

$$ 
  \small \newcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8} \begin{array}  {| c ||  c | c |}
  \hline
  \hspace{0.6cm} Type \hspace{0.6cm} & \hspace{1cm} Variable \hspace{1cm}  & \hspace{0.6cm} Notation \hspace{0.6cm}  \\
  \hline
  uint128  &{liquidityGross}        &{L_g}           \\
  \hline 
  int128   &{liquidityNet}           &{L_n}           \\
  \hline
  uint96  &{amountReceived}         &{R_r}            \\
  \hline
  uint96  &{amountOffset}           &{R_o}            \\
  \hline  
  uint32  &{tickHeight}             &{{H_t}}    \\
  \hline  
  uint32  &{activeEntryCount}       &{A_c}     \\
  \hline  
  bytes32[]  &{clearanceList}       &{C_t}     \\
  \hline  
  \end{array}
$$

$\small tickInfo$ may be in two different states: one in which its liquidity has not been traded at all, and one in which its liquidity has already been partially traded.

When the liquidity has not been traded at all, $\small L_g$ represents the total liquidity of all maker orders that use this $\small tick$ as a price boundary. $\small L_n$ represents the net liquidity of all maker orders that use this $\small tick$ as a price boundary, namely the change in liquidity when crossing this $\small tick$. In this state, $\small R_r$ and $\small R_o$ have no meaning and are both 0.

When the liquidity has been partially traded, $\small L_g$ represents the total liquidity of the price range above this $\small tick$, and $\small L_n$ represents the remaining net liquidity of that price range after liquidity rebalancing.

$\small R_r$ represents the amount of $\small token_1$ received after the liquidity is partially traded, and $\small R_o$ represents the sum of the internal exchange fee compensation and forward price-difference compensation contributed by all later maker orders.

$\small H_t$ is the lowest $\small poolHeight$ among all liquidity maker orders associated with this $\small tick$.

When the price crosses upward through a price range, $\small L_g, L_n, R_r, R_o,$ and $\small H_t$ in the lower-end $\small tick$ are all cleared. In the upper-end $\small tick$, $\small L_g, L_n, R_r, R_o,$ and $\small H_t$ are updated.

$\small C_t$ is the indexable clearance history of this $\small tick$. $\small A_c$ represents the number of clearance entries on this $\small tick$ that are still active. Even if the other trading-state fields of a $\small tick$ have been cleared, the $\small tick$ remains active as long as active historical clearance entries still exist.

### 4.3 Clearance List

The contract maintains a clearance list for each $\small tick$ that is crossed upward by the price. It is used to calculate the proceeds of the corresponding maker order when the order is closed.

$$
  \small \newcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8} \begin{array}  {| c ||  c | c |}
  \hline
  \hspace{0.6cm} Type \hspace{0.6cm} & \hspace{1cm} Variable \hspace{1cm}  & \hspace{0.6cm} Notation \hspace{0.6cm}  \\
  \hline
  uint128  &{liquiditySold}        &{L_s}           \\
  \hline
  uint96  &{amountReceived}        &{R_r}           \\
  \hline
  uint8  &{gap}  &{G_x}  \\
  \hline  
  uint24  &{crossHeight}  &{X_h}  \\
  \hline  
  \end{array}
$$

$\small L_s$ is the total liquidity sold in the current price range when the price crosses upward through this $\small tick$.

$\small R_r$ is the total amount of $\small token_1$ received from selling liquidity. Users share $\small R_r$ in proportion to the liquidity of their maker orders. The value of $\small R_r$ is the sum of the $\small token_1$ received from directly selling $\small token_0$ and the total internal-exchange compensation proceeds received.

$\small G_x$ represents the gap of this tick relative to the low tick boundary while this tick is crossed.

$\small X_h$ stores the low 24 bits of $\small poolHeight$ at the time of the upward crossing.

### 4.4 Maker Order Information

User maker orders include the following information:

$$
  \small \newcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8} \begin{array}  {| c ||  c | c |}
  \hline
  \hspace{0.6cm} Type \hspace{0.6cm} & \hspace{1cm} Variable \hspace{1cm}  & \hspace{0.6cm} Notation \hspace{0.6cm}  \\
  \hline
  uint128  &{liquidity}        &{L_p}           \\
  \hline
  uint32  &{orderHeight}         &{H_p}  \\
  \hline
  uint96  &{amountDeduction}    &{D_p}           \\
  \hline
  \end{array}
$$

$\small L_p$ is the liquidity of the maker order. $\small H_p$ is the $\small poolHeight$ of the trading pair when the maker order is placed. $\small D_p$ is the total compensation amount of the maker order, including the internal exchange compensation fee and the price-difference compensation fee. This amount is not an amount that the user actually pays when placing the maker order; instead, it is a virtual amount used to calculate the maker-order proceeds accurately while preventing unreasonable maker-order arbitrage. When the maker order is closed, this amount is deducted from the $\small token_1$ received by the user.

The price range of the maker order and the index information of the trading pair are both encoded into the mapping key of the maker-order record. The trading-pair contract itself does not need to store this information explicitly, but the Unibuy router contract needs to store and manage it.

## 5. Orders

When placing an order, the user must specify the two assets to be traded, $\small token_0$ and $\small token_1$, as well as the buy or sell price and amount. The Unibuy contract automatically maps the specified tokens and trading intent to the corresponding unidirectional trading pair and determines whether the price conditions allow the order to be processed as a taker order. If so, the order is filled immediately as a taker order; otherwise, it is processed as a maker order. If the order can only be partially filled, the filled portion is handled first as a taker order, and the remaining portion may either be canceled or placed as a maker order.

### 5.1 Maker Order

A maker order is an order that cannot be filled immediately and must instead be aggregated with the existing liquidity of the trading pair while waiting for future taker orders. Maker orders may trigger an internal exchange and the associated compensation processing within a price range, which may require the user to bear a certain amount of compensation in $\small token_1$. This compensation does not need to be paid upfront; instead, it is deducted from the amount received when the order is eventually settled. Generally even if a maker order is never filled, the amount received from the internal exchange is sufficient to cover the compensation. This compensation is not a loss to the user, but a value-balancing mechanism that prevents internal arbitrage opportunities.

The price range specified for a maker order must not exceed the corresponding price in the mirror trading pair. Any portion above that threshold could be filled immediately in the mirror pair, and the contract performs the corresponding checks.

### 5.2 Close Order

A user may close a maker order at any time, regardless of whether it is unfilled, partially filled, or fully filled.

Even if a maker order has never been filled, the amount of $\small token_0$ the user receives upon closure may differ from the amount originally deposited. If the order has triggered the internal exchange and the forward compensation mechanism, the user may receive a reduced amount of $\small token_0$ together with a certain amount of $\small token_1$.

If a maker order has been fully filled, the user is expected to close it promptly within one week. If it is closed within that one-week period, no fee is charged. After one week, the Unibuy protocol charges a delayed-closing fee, paid in $\small token_1$; at the same time, the protocol allows third parties to close the order on behalf of the user, in which case the user may need to pay a closing service fee, typically $\small 0.1\%$ of the received $\small token_1$. The Unibuy protocol team may also provide this service. This mechanism prevents large numbers of fully filled maker orders from accumulating in the contract for a long time and thereby affecting the performance of maker-order creation and closure.

### 5.3 Taker Order

When an order can be partially or fully filled immediately, it is processed as a taker order. A taker order must pay a transaction fee, typically $\small 0.3\%$, denominated in $\small token_1$. Taker orders are always filled immediately. If an order cannot be filled completely at once, the user may choose one of the following handling modes:  
  1. Partially fill the order and cancel the remainder.
  2. Partially fill the order and place the remainder as a maker order.
  3. Do not fill the order at all and cancel it entirely.

### 5.4 Order Chaining

Order chaining allows a user, when placing a maker order, to specify that if the order is fully filled, the received $\small token_1$ may be treated as $\small token_0$ in the mirror trading pair and placed as a new maker order within a specified price range.

Order chaining is not executed automatically. It must be triggered by the user, a third-party service provider, or the Unibuy protocol team. If it is triggered by someone other than the user, a fee might be charged.

## 6. Summary

The Unibuy protocol is a significant evolution of constant-product-AMM DEX design. It addresses issues present in existing DEXs, including sandwich attacks and impermanent loss. Traditional market makers can still participate in Unibuy by placing maker orders and taker orders, but at the protocol level their trading behavior is treated the same as that of ordinary traders, and the protocol no longer shares trading fees with market makers. This allows the Unibuy project to earn protocol revenue from trading fees and thereby support its own sustainable development. Ordinary users can place idle assets for sale and, through maker-order chaining, automatically implement buy-low/sell-high strategies by shifting liquidity to the mirror pair after an order is fully filled. If large numbers of individual users participate in this kind of arbitrage, it can help dampen market volatility while also generating passive income for ordinary users.

The Unibuy protocol offers an order-book experience similar to that of centralized exchanges without requiring users to place their assets in centralized custody, thereby eliminating custody risk. Due to the latency and discontinuity inherent in blockchain transactions, placing a maker order should be a deliberate act that reflects the user’s true intention. Maker orders are transparently visible on-chain, which helps avoid misleading practices seen in some CEXs, such as repeatedly placing and canceling orders, self-trading, and price manipulation.

DEX protocols will continue to evolve. The Unibuy protocol represents an exploratory but meaningful advance in that evolution, and more innovations in DEX design will undoubtedly emerge in the future.
